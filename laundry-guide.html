The Laundry Guide

Sorting, temperature, detergent, drying and the machine maintenance nobody mentions until something smells.

Laundry is the single biggest cause of wear in a wardrobe, and almost all of that damage is avoidable. This guide covers one ordinary machine, one cupboard of detergent and the decisions that actually matter.

Sort by three things, not one

Colour is the sort everyone does and the least important of the three. Sort first by how much heat the load can take, then by how rough the items are on each other, then by colour. A zip in with a knit will do more damage in one cycle than a stray red sock ever did.

In practice that gives you four piles: everyday cottons, delicates and knitwear, towels and bedding, and anything heavily soiled. Four piles sounds like a lot until you realise most households already run four loads a week.

Temperature

Thirty degrees cleans ordinary clothes. Detergent enzymes are designed for it, and the mechanical action of the drum does most of the work. Sixty degrees is for towels, bedding and anything that has been near illness, where you want hygiene rather than stain removal. Ninety is for nothing you own.

Detergent, and how much is too much

Most people use roughly double what they need, which leaves residue in the cloth, holds odour and gradually stiffens towels. Liquid suits cool washes and dark colours; powder contains whitening agents that work better warm and is cheaper per load. Soft water needs considerably less of either.

If your clothes feel slightly stiff out of the machine, you are using too much detergent, not too little.

Drying

Line drying costs nothing and is gentler than any tumble setting. Hang shirts on hangers and smooth the seams while damp, which removes most of the need to iron. Dry knitwear flat on a towel so the weight of the water does not stretch the shoulders. If you tumble dry, use low heat and take things out slightly damp.

Pressing

Steam does the work; weight just flattens. Press on the reverse of dark fabrics to avoid shine, use a cloth over prints and anything delicate, and work from the smallest parts outwards: collar, cuffs, sleeves, then the body. A garment steamer handles almost everything a shirt-wearer needs and is kinder to fabric than an iron.

Looking after the machine

A washing machine that smells will make clean clothes smell. Once a month, run an empty cycle at the highest temperature with a machine cleaner or plain white vinegar, wipe the rubber door seal including underneath the lip, and pull the detergent drawer right out and scrub it. Leave the door ajar between washes so the drum can dry.

A simple weekly routine

Sort as you undress rather than in one pile on a Saturday. Treat marks the day they happen, using the Stain Guide. Run loads three-quarters full at the temperature the Wash Load Planner suggests. Hang everything you can. Press only what shows. That is the whole of it, and it will add years to a wardrobe.
